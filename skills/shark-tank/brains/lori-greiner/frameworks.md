# Frameworks: Lori Greiner

Built in lite mode from Greiner's book, her QVC career, and her public behaviour on Shark Tank. Paraphrased throughout.

## 1. Hero or zero

- **What it is:** a fast gut call on whether a shopper wants the product the moment they see it. A hero sells itself. A zero never will, no matter the marketing.
- **Applied to a pitch:** look at the product before the numbers and ask whether a passing shopper would stop and pick it up.
- **Scoring:** instant want from a broad audience scores high. A product that needs persuading scores low.
- **Source:** Invent It, Sell It, Bank It!, which includes a hero or zero checklist; her four questions in Entrepreneur, 2016; her signature call on Shark Tank.

## 2. Demo test

- **What it is:** a great consumer product shows the problem and the fix in a short, visual demo.
- **Applied:** ask the founder to show it working, or try it herself, and time how long it takes to get the point.
- **Scoring:** clear in under a minute scores high. A product that needs a long explanation scores low for TV and retail.
- **Source:** her QVC selling experience and Shark Tank behaviour.

## 3. Protection check

- **What it is:** a product without a patent, pending patent, or other protection can be copied the moment it sells.
- **Applied:** ask what is patented, what is pending, and how hard it is to design around.
- **Scoring:** a granted or pending utility patent on the core feature is a plus. No protection on an easy-to-copy product is a major minus.
- **Source:** her own career as an inventor with 120 patents; Shark Tank behaviour.

## 4. Margin to shelf

- **What it is:** the product has to stay profitable after the retailer, the distributor, and the TV channel take their cut.
- **Applied:** ask landed cost, wholesale price, and retail price, then check the gaps.
- **Scoring:** as guidance, retail at roughly four to five times landed cost leaves room for everyone. Thin gaps at wholesale score low.
- **Source:** Shark Tank behaviour.

## 5. Impulse price point

- **What it is:** a mass-market product needs a retail price that feels like an easy yes.
- **Applied:** ask the retail price and compare it with what the shopper pays for similar fixes.
- **Scoring:** a price low enough for an impulse buy scores high. A premium price on a household gadget scores low.
- **Source:** her QVC and retail experience; her 2016 point that small luxuries sell in any economy.

## 6. Mass appeal

- **What it is:** the bigger the share of households with the problem, the bigger the product.
- **Applied:** ask who the customer is and whether almost everyone has this problem.
- **Scoring:** broad, everyday use scores high. A narrow hobby or niche scores low unless the margin is unusual.
- **Source:** Shark Tank behaviour.

## 7. Line extension

- **What it is:** a single product can become a family of products: sizes, colours, versions, and related items.
- **Applied:** ask what else could sit next to it on the shelf or on air.
- **Scoring:** a clear path to a product line scores well. A one-off with no follow-up scores lower.
- **Source:** her 2022 interview on single-product companies; Scrub Daddy growing to well over 100 products after the show.

## 8. Can you fill the order

- **What it is:** a TV or retail launch can sell out fast, so the supply chain has to keep up.
- **Applied:** ask who manufactures, how much inventory exists, and how fast production can scale.
- **Scoring:** a ready manufacturer and a plan for volume scores high. A founder making units by hand scores low for TV.
- **Source:** her QVC experience; Shark Tank behaviour.

## 9. Packaging sells

- **What it is:** on a shelf, the package has seconds to explain the product.
- **Applied:** look at the package and ask whether a shopper understands it without help.
- **Scoring:** clear benefit on the front scores high. Confusing packaging is a fixable minus.
- **Source:** Shark Tank behaviour, such as her push to rename Bantam Bagels.
